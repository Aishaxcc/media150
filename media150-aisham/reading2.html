In “With ‘AI slop’ distorting our reality, the world is sleepwalking into disaster,” Nesrine Malik argues that the danger of AI-generated content is not only that it is fake, but that it is becoming so common that people are slowly getting used to distortion. Her article focuses on what she calls “AI slop,” which includes low-quality AI-generated images, videos, and posts that flood social media and messaging spaces. What stood out to me most is her idea that this content does not have to be believable in a perfect way to still be harmful. Even when it looks strange, exaggerated, or obviously artificial, it can still influence people emotionally and politically because of how quickly it spreads and how often people see it.

I think Malik’s point is especially important because she connects AI slop to a larger problem about reality itself. Online, people already move through huge amounts of information without stopping to question every image or headline. When AI content becomes part of that environment, it becomes even harder to tell what is real, manipulated, staged, or completely invented. Malik suggests that this does not just confuse people in isolated moments. Instead, it changes the overall atmosphere of digital life. People become less shocked by fake content, less motivated to verify what they see, and more likely to absorb distorted ideas without realizing it.

Another strong part of the article is the way Malik describes the relationship between AI content and platform culture. Social media platforms reward speed, reaction, and attention. AI-generated material fits perfectly into that system because it can be produced quickly, in large quantities, and with emotional appeal. That means the issue is not only the technology itself, but also the online systems that encourage this kind of content to spread. I agree with her argument that these platforms benefit from confusion and overstimulation. If users stay engaged, the platform still wins, even if the content is misleading, shallow, or harmful.

The article also made me think about desensitization. Malik’s warning about “sleepwalking into disaster” feels powerful because it suggests people are adapting to a damaged information environment without fully recognizing what is happening. Instead of rejecting manipulation, many users are becoming familiar with it. That numbness can be dangerous in moments when real political issues, violence, or injustice require urgency and clarity. If people are constantly surrounded by synthetic images and emotionally manipulative content, they may become less responsive to serious events in the real world.

Overall, I found Malik’s article convincing because it treats AI slop as more than just annoying internet clutter. She frames it as a cultural and political issue that affects trust, attention, and public understanding. I agree with her because the problem is not simply that fake content exists, but that it is becoming normalized. The more normal distortion feels, the easier it becomes for people to accept confusion as everyday life. That is what makes the article effective: it shows that AI-generated media is not only changing what we see online, but also changing how we relate to truth itself.
